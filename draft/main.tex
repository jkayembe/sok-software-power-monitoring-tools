\section{Introduction \& Motivation}

The energy consumption of software systems has attracted increasing attention in recent years. Digital services continue to scale across a wide range of computing contexts, from mobile and embedded devices to large-scale cloud infrastructures. They are increasingly recognised as not only being passive consumers of hardware resources, but also direct determinants of system-level power and energy demand. Therefore, the ability to measure and reason about software-induced energy consumption is crucial for energy-aware software engineering, and green system design.

Over the past decade, diverse tools have emerged to measure or estimate the power and energy consumption of software. These tools can differ substantially in their underlying measurement principles, abstraction level and intended use cases. Some tools rely on external power meters or hardware-exposed energy interfaces provided by modern processors, e.g. Intel RAPL or Nvidia NVLM, while others estimate energy consumption using models that may be trained or theoretically designed to estimate system power based on hardware performance counters, operating system statistics, or analytical relations. Tools also vary in the granularity at which energy is attributed, ranging from whole-system measurements to per-process, per-virtual-machine, or even per-function estimates. This diversity directly reflects the multitude of use-case scenarios, but also makes it difficult to understand the trade-offs involved and to select appropriate tools for a given context.

Existing literature focuses on proposing new tools or evaluating individual techniques in isolation. While several surveys exist \cite{wysockiMethodsImprovingSoftware2025}, they tend to catalogue tools or measurement methods without providing a unifying structure that explains why certain approaches are suitable for specific environments and goals, and why others are not. Additionally, the increasing use of virtualisation and cloud-native environments introduces new complexities such as limited hardware visibility, multi-tenancy, and the lack of access to physical power measurements that are not systematically addressed in existing measurement approaches.

In this paper, we present a Systematisation of Knowledge (SoK) on software power and energy measurement tools. While we do not aim for an exhaustive survey, our goal is to organise existing tools and approaches along dimensions that capture their main design choices, operational trade-offs, and practical applicability.

More specifically, we systematise profiling tools according to dimensions including:
\begin{itemize}

    \item \emph{Granularity}: what software entities are attributed energy consumption,

    \item \emph{Measurement sources}: how energy and activity information are obtained,

    \item \emph{Allocation model}: how system-level energy is distributed across software entities,

    \item \emph{Virtualised environment support}: how profiling is supported in virtualised and cloud-native infrastructures,

    \item \emph{Operational constraints}: accuracy, temporal resolution, runtime overhead, code modification requirement, privilege requirements, portability, and platform dependencies,

    \item \emph{Maturity and ecosystem}: deployment complexity, availability and licensing, validation, maintenance status, documentation quality, case studies, community adoption, and additional metrics provided by the tool.

\end{itemize}

This systematisation exposes common patterns, implicit assumptions, and trade-offs that are often obscured when tools are considered individually.

The contributions of this SoK are threefold. First, we propose a taxonomy of software energy measurement approaches that clarifies the design space and provides a shared vocabulary for future work. Secondly, we derive a decision framework that supports researchers and practitioners in selecting measurement tools based on their experimental goals and operational constraints. Third, through a comparative analysis of representative tools, we identify open challenges and research gaps. By consolidating and structuring existing knowledge, this paper aims to serve as a foundation for both empirical research and tool development in software energy measurement.

\section{Related Work}

Several studies focus on individual software energy profiling tools. These studies are valuable because they provide detailed insights into the design, capabilities, and limitations of specific profilers, which we later use to position them within our taxonomy. Examples include evaluations of Kepler's suitability for cloud power observability \cite{bellalInvestigatingPotentialKepler}, the Green Metrics Tool \cite{hoffmannGreenMetricsTool2025}, e-Surgeon for diagnosing energy leaks in application servers \cite{noureddineESurgeonDiagnosingEnergy2012}, and CNCF's review of cloud-native sustainability tooling \cite{cncfExploringKeplersPotentials2023}. Similarly, Noureddine \cite{noureddinePowerJoularJoularJXMultiPlatform2022} presents PowerJoular and JoularJX, highlighting their multi-platform support and demonstrating how software energy measurements can be used to compare programming language choices across platforms. Di Nucci et al. \cite{dinucciSoftwarebasedEnergyProfiling2017} discuss the energy profiling capabilities of IgProf and its integration into software optimisation workflows.

A second category of work compares a limited number of tools or measurement approaches to investigate specific design choices. Centofanti et al. \cite{centofantiImpactPowerConsumption2024} evaluate several techniques for monitoring power consumption in cloud environments, including Kepler, Scaphandre, external smart power meters, and system-level measurements. Their results show that measured consumption can vary significantly depending on the selected monitoring approach. Raffin and Trystram \cite{raffinDissectingSoftwarebasedMeasurement2024} compare multiple mechanisms for accessing RAPL measurements, including direct Model-Specific Register (MSR) access, operating-system interfaces, and eBPF. Their study highlights important trade-offs regarding measurement latency, implementation complexity, and overhead. Notably, they conclude that higher-level interfaces such as perf-events often provide the best balance between efficiency and usability, while excessive sampling frequencies may significantly increase energy consumption by preventing processors from entering low-power states.

The most closely related studies attempt to provide broader overviews of available energy measurement and monitoring tools. Wysocki et al. \cite{wysockiMethodsImprovingSoftware2025} survey methods for improving software energy efficiency and identify profiling tools as essential instruments for quantifying optimisation gains. Jagannadharao et al. \cite{jagannadharaoBeginnersGuidePower2024} provide a comprehensive introduction to power and energy measurement for computing systems and machine learning workloads, including a catalogue of available measurement and estimation tools. Zoni et al. \cite{zoniSurveyRuntimePower2023} survey run-time power monitoring approaches for edge systems and propose a taxonomy of hardware-level power estimation techniques. Their work focuses primarily on the generation of hardware power metrics, whereas our study focuses on how software profiling tools allocate hardware energy consumption to software entities.

The work most closely related to ours is the review by Noureddine et al. \cite{noureddineReviewEnergyMeasurement2013} in 2013. The authors compare energy measurement approaches according to dimensions such as monitored resources, energy precision, operating-system modifications, software modifications, and hardware requirements. Several of these dimensions correspond directly to dimensions in our taxonomy, including profiling granularity, deployment complexity, code modification requirements, and the use of external power meters. Our work builds upon this foundation while extending the analysis to modern profiling tools and techniques. In particular, many profilers available today rely on hardware power interfaces such as RAPL, which were not yet widely available when the review was conducted and therefore were not a major consideration in the surveyed approaches.

To the best of our knowledge, existing surveys either focus on individual tools, compare a limited set of monitoring approaches, or provide catalogues of available solutions. None systematically characterise the design space of software energy profilers in terms of the dimensions that determine their applicability, trade-offs, and suitability for different usage contexts. This gap motivates the need for a systematisation of knowledge that explains not only which tools exist, but also why different classes of tools are required.


\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Profiler selection}

The objective of this study is not to produce an exhaustive catalogue of existing software energy profiling tools, but rather to analyse a representative set covering the main measurement approaches currently adopted in the literature and in practice. To keep the study tractable while covering the breadth of the design space, the analysis was intentionally limited to twelve tools.

In particular, seed tools were selected based on:
\begin{itemize}
    \item prior use in peer-reviewed academic publications,
    \item availability of public documentation or source code,
    \item relevance to contemporary execution environments, including virtualised and containerised systems.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\textbf{Seed selection}}

The selection process was initiated from three well-established seed tools representing different parts of the software energy profiling landscape: \emph{Kepler} \cite{centofantiImpactPowerConsumption2024,amaralKeplerFrameworkCalculate2023,bellalInvestigatingPotentialKepler}, \emph{Green Metrics Tool} \cite{hoffmannGreenMetricsTool2025,kayembeExploringPrivacySecurity2025}, and \emph{e-Surgeon} \cite{noureddineMonitoringEnergyHotspots2015}. These tools were chosen because they are well documented, frequently referenced in the literature, and representative of different measurement and attribution strategies.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Inclusion criteria}}

Candidate tools were included if they satisfied all of the following criteria:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Scientific grounding.} The tool is described in a peer-reviewed publication or a sufficiently documented technical report, allowing its measurement methodology and assumptions to be analysed.
    
    \item \emph{Software energy profiling.} The tool provides energy or power measurements attributable to software entities (e.g., process, container or application), rather than only exposing hardware measurements.

    \item \emph{Representative execution environments.} The tool targets contemporary computing environments, including bare-metal systems, virtual machines, containers or cloud platforms.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\textbf{Exclusion criteria}}

Candidate tools were excluded if they met one or more of the following conditions:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Insufficient documentation.} The measurement methodology, assumptions or evaluation could not be established from publicly available sources.

    \item \emph{Mobile-only platforms.} The tool exclusively targets smartphones or mobile operating systems, as this study focuses on general-purpose computing and cloud environments.

    \item \emph{Obsolete environments.} The tool targets deprecated hardware platforms or software stacks with limited relevance to current systems.

    \item \emph{No publicly available implementation.} The work proposes a measurement approach without an implemented tool or empirical evaluation.
\end{itemize}

\subsubsection{\textbf{Snowballing strategy}}

To reduce the bias introduced by the initial seed selection and improve the representativeness of the final dataset, we applied iterative backward and forward snowballing. For each selected tool, we examined:

\begin{itemize}
    \item references cited by the original publication,
    \item publications citing the tool,
    \item comparative studies discussing related software energy profiling tools.
\end{itemize}

Candidate tools identified through snowballing were evaluated against the same inclusion and exclusion criteria. The process was repeated until no additional representative tools satisfying the criteria were identified or the target set of twelve tools had been reached.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Outcome of the selection process}}

The resulting dataset comprises twelve representative software energy profiling tools covering different measurement sources, attribution mechanisms, granularities and execution environments. Each tool is characterised using the taxonomy proposed in Section~\ref{taxonomy} and summarised in a comparative table. This analysis forms the basis for identifying the strengths, limitations and design trade-offs of current software energy profiling approaches.

\subsection{Taxonomy construction}

Our objective was not to define arbitrary characteristics for comparing tools, but to construct a taxonomy that captures the principal design choices shaping software energy profiling. To achieve this, we first identified the recurring challenges reported in the literature and then defined a set of observable dimensions describing how different tools address these challenges.

The development of the taxonomy was conducted iteratively alongside the tool selection process. As we identified candidate tools and examined their publications, documentation and source code, we progressively extracted recurring design choices, implementation strategies and trade-offs. Whenever we observed that a particular aspect significantly influenced the performance, the limits or implementation of several tools, we introduced it as a taxonomy dimension. Conversely, characteristics that did not meaningfully distinguish tools or were specific to a single implementation were discarded.

This iterative process allowed the selection of representative tools, the identification of profiling challenges, and the refinement of the taxonomy to mutually inform one another. As our understanding of existing approaches evolved, both the set of analysed tools and the taxonomy itself were refined. The resulting taxonomy is therefore grounded in the practical design decisions observed across representative software energy profiling tools and remains structured around the fundamental challenges discussed in the following section.


\section{Profiling Challenges} \label{challenges}

Energy profiling of software systems is inherently indirect. While hardware components consume energy, software only influences this consumption through its use of hardware resources. Consequently, profiling tools do not measure software energy consumption directly, but instead estimate hardware energy and attribute it to software based on observable signals and modelling assumptions. In its most general form, the energy attributed to software entity $i$ can be expressed as

\begin{equation}
E_i = A_i * E_{\mathrm{hardware}},
\label{eq:general-attribution}
\end{equation}

where $E_{\mathrm{hardware}}$ denotes the energy consumed by a hardware component over the considered time interval, and $A_i$ represents the estimated share of this energy attributable to software entity $i$.

Most approaches can therefore be abstracted as a combination of two sources of information:
\emph{(i) hardware-level energy estimation}, which determines $E_{\mathrm{hardware}}$ from hardware interfaces, external measurements, or software models, and
\emph{(ii) software activity share estimation}, which estimates $A_i$ from observable software activity signals.
Profiling then consists in combining these two sources to reconstruct an attribution of hardware energy consumption to software entities. This process raises several challenges.

\subsection{Allocation Model}

The intrinsic challenge is to define a model that maps observable resource utilisation metrics to the actual share of hardware energy consumed by software entities. The quality of this allocation model depends on three main design choices: the software entities to which energy is attributed, the observable signals used to estimate their share of activity, and the assumptions made to translate this activity into energy shares.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Allocation granularity}}

A first design choice consists in defining the software entities to which hardware energy will be attributed. Depending on the intended use case, profilers may target:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Processes}, providing the energy consumption of individual software processes;

    \item \emph{Groups of processes}, such as an application, virtual machines, containers, or other workload abstractions, providing the energy consumption of a set of processes;

    \item \emph{Threads or Code sections}, such as functions or methods, providing fine-grained insight into the internal energy behaviour of a process.
\end{itemize}

The selected granularity determines the observability requirements and the complexity of the allocation model. Finer granularities provide more insight for software optimisation, but require additional observability and stronger modelling assumptions.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Activity reconstruction}} \label{activity reconstruction}

Once the target software entities have been defined, the profiler must estimate their relative share of hardware activity in order to distribute the measured hardware energy.

For processes or groups of processes, operating systems and orchestration frameworks already expose accounting information describing resource usage over time. However, this information only provides an indirect view of the actual hardware activity and therefore requires modelling assumptions to translate resource utilisation into energy shares. For instance, allocating CPU energy proportionally to the CPU time reported by the operating system implicitly assumes that CPU time is a good proxy for its energy consumption. This assumption becomes less accurate when concurrent processes execute different mixes of instructions or operate at different processor frequencies, resulting in different energy costs for the same CPU time.

Estimating activity shares at finer granularities, such as threads or code sections, is even more challenging because operating systems generally provide no direct visibility at this level. Profilers must therefore reconstruct software activity shares from additional observations obtained during execution. Some mechanisms require manual or automatic instrumentation of the code to insert execution markers delimiting execution intervals. These markers enable process-level energy to be distributed across code sections, but introduce code modification requirements.

Consequently, different profiling tools rely on different activity reconstruction mechanisms, each making different assumptions regarding software execution and hardware resource sharing. While the type of activity reconstruction used depends on the intended granularity, it directly influences attribution accuracy, runtime overhead, and deployment complexity.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Baseline consumption}}

Finally, profiling tools must decide how to handle baseline system consumption. Since operating systems, background services, and idle hardware consume energy independently of the monitored application, tools differ in whether they subtract this baseline or distribute it among the monitored entities. This choice affects attribution results, particularly in lightly loaded contexts.

\subsection{Access to Measurement Sources}

As discussed previously, profiling tools must collect two categories of information: hardware-level energy estimations and software activity share estimations. A second challenge consists in accessing these sources reliably.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Platform dependencies}}

Profiling tools are also constrained by the operating systems and execution environments they target. Many measurement interfaces, performance counters, or privilege mechanisms are operating-system specific, limiting the portability of profiling approaches across platforms. As a result, some tools are specifically tied to a given operating-system, others implement platform-specific backends to support multiple environments through a unified profiling interface, while model-based approaches trade accuracy for improved portability by reducing their dependence on platform-specific measurement sources.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Privilege constraints}}

Access to measurement sources can be restricted for security reasons. In particular, some hardware counters and energy registers have been shown to enable side-channel attacks \todo{Cite a few attacks through RAPL and mitigations}, leading operating systems and hardware vendors to restrict or filter access to these interfaces.

\begin{itemize}
    \item hardware counters and energy registers may require elevated privileges,
    \item hardware-level metrics exposed to user-space applications may be partially filtered, aggregated, or sampled by the operating system.
\end{itemize}

As a result, profiling tools differ in how they access these sources. Some rely on operating-system-exposed interfaces available from user space. Others use privileged mechanisms such as eBPF-based instrumentation—a method to securely run user code within the kernel—to access lower-level metrics directly. These design choices can affect accuracy, temporal resolution, platform compatibility and profiling overhead.

\subsection{Temporal Resolution}

Energy profiling relies on periodically observing system activity and updating energy attribution estimates over time. The frequency at which these observations and attribution routines are executed directly impacts profiling quality. Regardless of the underlying profiling mechanism, increasing the observation frequency generally improves temporal precision hence attribution accuracy. However, it also increases runtime overhead and sensitivity to noise. Conversely, lower observation frequencies reduce profiling overhead but may fail to capture short-lived execution events. Temporal resolution therefore defines a trade-off between accuracy and measurement overhead.

\subsection{Profiling Overhead}

Profiling mechanisms introduce runtime overhead. This overhead arises from \emph{(i) instrumentation of code or binaries}, \emph{(ii) periodic sampling and interrupt handling} and \emph{(iii) live logging of estimated energy shares}. It generally increases with the profiler's complexity. Most observation mechanisms must execute online during the monitored program's execution. However, some approaches defer part of the data processing and analysis offline, reducing runtime overhead when live analysis is not required. In any case, measurements may be biased by the profiling process itself. Minimising and quantifying this overhead is necessary to ensure the validity of observations, yet it is not always explicitly addressed.

\subsection{Virtualisation and Multi-Tenancy}

Modern execution environments introduce additional complexity through virtualisation and resource sharing. Applications often execute inside containers or virtual machines, themselves managed by container runtimes, hypervisors, or orchestration platforms, creating multiple layers between the software being profiled and the underlying hardware.

This layering creates several issues:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{limited visibility}, as hardware measurement sources may not be accessible from within virtualised environments,
    \item \emph{blind resource sharing}, where multiple entities share the same hardware components, not necessarily being aware of it,
    \item \emph{attribution ambiguity}, particularly for the energy consumed by the virtualisation layer itself.
\end{itemize}

Virtualisation therefore introduces additional observability requirements. Since hardware-level measurements are generally available only at the host level, guest environments may lack the information required to perform accurate energy attribution. Profiling in virtualised environments consequently depends on the visibility provided by the underlying infrastructure, which is not always available without explicit support from the host environment.

\subsection{Synthesis}

These challenges highlight that software energy profiling fundamentally consists in reconstructing a mapping between \emph{hardware-level energy measurements} and \emph{software activity shares}, under partial observability and shared resource constraints. The previous subsections show that the design choices adopted by profiling tools determine their position within an operational constraint space characterised by several competing objectives:

\begin{itemize}

    \item \emph{Accuracy}, depending on the quality and temporal resolution of the selected measurement sources, as well as the validity of the attribution assumptions,

    \item \emph{Deployability}, characterised by hardware requirements, platform dependencies, privilege constraints, and code modification requirements,

    \item \emph{Profiling overhead}, resulting from the execution of the profiler itself and increasing with its complexity.

    \item \emph{Support for virtualised and multi-tenant environments}, depending on the visibility provided across software abstraction layers.

\end{itemize}

Improving one aspect can often impact the others. For instance, increasing attribution granularity generally requires richer activity reconstruction mechanisms and higher observation frequencies, which increase profiling overhead and may require code instrumentation. Similarly, approaches relying on low-level hardware observability may improve attribution accuracy while reducing portability and deployability due to platform dependencies and privilege requirements.

Profiling tools can therefore be understood as different combinations of design choices they make to address these challenges, each inducing a position within the operational constraint space. In the next section we formalise these design choices into a taxonomy and relate them to the operational constraints they induce, enabling a structured comparison of existing profiling approaches.

\section{Taxonomy of software energy profiling tools} \label{taxonomy}

In the previous section we identified the fundamental challenges of software energy profiling and showed that existing approaches correspond to different compromises within the resulting operational constraint space.

The objective of the proposed taxonomy is to formalise the methodological choices through which profiling tools address these challenges into dimensions enabling a systematic comparison. Rather than cataloguing profiler features, we classify tools according to how they estimate hardware-level energy, reconstruct software activity shares, and attribute energy consumption to software entities.

These methodological choices determine the assumptions, capabilities, and limitations of each profiler, and consequently its position within the operational constraints space. Accordingly, we first describe the possible \emph{(i) design space} available to profiling tools. We then introduce the \emph{(ii) operational constraints space} used to assess the trade-offs induced by these choices, before finally describing the \emph{(iii) maturity and ecosystem} dimensions used to complement the comparison.

\subsection{Design space}

We first characterise the principal methodological choices that distinguish existing software energy profiling approaches.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Granularity}}

This design choice describes the software abstraction level to which energy consumption is attributed. Profiling tools may operate at \emph{(i) virtual machine}, \emph{(ii) pod}, \emph{(iii) container}, \emph{(iv) application}, \emph{(v) process}, \emph{(vi) thread}, or \emph{(vii) code-section} level.

\begin{itemize}
    \item Process-level attribution is the most common profiling granularity and often the first step in providing other levels of granularities. These approaches generally rely on operating-system scheduling and accounting statistics, such as per-process CPU time or other resource utilisation, to distribute hardware-level energy consumption among processes. Their accuracy therefore depends both on the quality of the underlying operating-system resource accounting mechanisms and on the assumptions used to distribute shared hardware consumption across concurrent workloads.
    \item Virtual-machine-, pod-, and container-level attribution are mainly designed for cloud-native and virtualised environments, where these entities represent the workload units managed by infrastructure operators and orchestration frameworks. Profilers supporting these granularities generally rely on isolation mechanisms such as cgroups or hypervisors to group processes belonging to the same workload. In practice, energy attribution is first performed at process level, before aggregating the energy shares of the processes associated with the same virtual machine, container, or pod.
    \item Thread-level attribution provides finer insight inside processes by attempting to distribute process-level energy consumption among threads. Achieving this granularity requires a first level of process energy attribution, and access to thread scheduling and execution statistics, generally through operating-system or runtime-level observability tools.
    \item Code-section-level profiling is more dedicated to software engineering and optimisation use cases. These approaches attempt to attribute energy consumption to functions, methods, or code regions. As for thread-level attribution, they typically rely on two layers of attribution: the profiler must first allocate energy consumption to the monitored process before distributing this process-level energy across internal code sections. This finer decomposition requires additional instrumentation mechanisms such as statistical call-stack sampling or execution markers. Consequently, fine-grained attribution increases implementation complexity hence runtime overhead and may require code modification.
\end{itemize}


\subsubsection{\textbf{Measurment Sources}}

While the hardware-level energy to allocate theoretically corresponds to the energy consumption of the whole machine, profiling tools differ in how they decompose this consumption into hardware domains. Some estimate only total system energy, while others distinguish domains such as the CPU, DRAM, GPU, or network interfaces.

This decomposition provides insight into which hardware resources are responsible for the energy consumption attributed to software entities, helping identify optimisation opportunities. For example, some applications are primarily CPU and memory intensive, while others are dominated by GPU computation or network transfers.

Each hardware domain may rely on different hardware-level energy estimation and software activity share proxy. For instance, a profiler may estimate CPU and DRAM energy using hardware-exposed interfaces while relying on models for GPU or network energy. Likewise, software activity shares may be reconstructed from different proxies depending on the monitored component, such as CPU utilisation, memory-access counters, GPU runtime statistics, or network throughput.

The taxonomy therefore characterises, independently for the CPU, DRAM, GPU, network interfaces, and system-level consumption, both the hardware-level energy estimation and the software activity share proxy used by a profiler.

\paragraph{\textbf{Hardware-level energy estimation}}

As discussed in Section~\ref{challenges}, profiling first requires an estimate of the energy consumed by the monitored hardware domains. Existing tools mainly rely on:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{External power meters}, which directly measure the electrical power consumed by the hardware using dedicated instrumentation. They provide the highest measurement fidelity and are commonly used for validation, but require dedicated equipment, physical access, and synchronisation with software activity observations.

    \item \emph{Hardware-exposed interfaces}, implemented by hardware vendors to estimate the energy consumed by domains such as the CPU, DRAM, GPU, or sometimes the whole system. These measurements are exposed through dedicated registers accessed via operating-system interfaces, kernel drivers, or vendor APIs. They generally provide low-overhead, high-frequency capability, but remain dependent on hardware vendors, operating systems, and privilege restrictions.

    \item \emph{Software models}, which estimate energy from observable metrics such as processor frequency, CPU utilisation, performance counters, or other system activity metrics. These models may be analytical or pre-trained from reference measurements. They improve portability and compatibility with virtualised environments, but introduce modelling assumptions and calibration requirements that may reduce accuracy.
\end{itemize}

The selected energy estimation mechanism therefore directly influences the trade-off between measurement accuracy, deployability, and portability.

\paragraph{\textbf{Software activity share proxies}}

Software activity cannot be observed directly. Profiling tools therefore rely on observable signals, that we refer to as \emph{activity share proxies}, to estimate the fraction of hardware activity attributable to each software entity. These proxies constitute the inputs of the allocation model and are expected to correlate with the actual use of the monitored hardware resource.

The choice of proxy depends both on the monitored hardware component and on the desired attribution granularity. Different profiling stages may even rely on different proxies. For example, a profiler may first estimate the CPU activity share of each process from operating-system statistics before further distributing the process energy among code sections using call-stack sampling or execution markers as proxies.

Common proxies for activity shares include:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \emph{Operating-system accounting statistics}, such as CPU utilisation, memory accesses, or network throughput, typically used for process-level attribution.

    \item \emph{Hardware performance counters (HPCs)}, exposing low-level hardware events such as retired instructions, cache accesses, or CPU cycles through dedicated processor registers. These proxies may better capture hardware activity than operating-system statistics under some workloads. However, collecting and processing them may increase implementation complexity and runtime overhead, while reducing portability due to their hardware-specific nature and, in some cases, requiring privileged access.

    \item \emph{Call stacks}, periodically sampled to estimate the relative execution time of functions or methods.

    \item \emph{Hardware-driver runtime statistics}, for hardware components exposing per-process activity information, such as GPU driver APIs.

    \item \emph{Execution markers}, explicitly delimiting execution intervals through manual or automatic instrumentation.
\end{itemize}

Regardless of the selected proxy, the underlying assumption is that the observed signal is sufficiently correlated with the actual share of hardware activity. The validity of this assumption depends on both the hardware component and the workload. For instance, allocating CPU energy proportionally to CPU time implicitly assumes that all concurrent workloads have a similar energy cost per unit of CPU time, whereas instruction-based proxies may better capture differences in instruction mix or processor frequency.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Allocation model}}

\paragraph{\textbf{Activity share estimation}}

Once software activity proxies have been collected, profiling tools differ in how they estimate the share of hardware activity attributable to software entities before distributing the corresponding energy consumption. Existing approaches mainly rely on four estimation strategies. Regardless of the strategy, the objective is to estimate the activity share $A_i$ of software entity $i$, such that

\begin{equation}
A_i \in [0,1]
\end{equation}

from which the attributed energy is obtained as

\begin{equation}
E_i = E_{\mathrm{tot}} A_i,
\end{equation}

where $E_i$ denotes the energy attributed to software entity $i$ and $E_{\mathrm{tot}}$ the measured energy of the monitored hardware component.

\begin{itemize}

    \item \emph{Direct proportional allocation} assumes that the selected activity proxy already represents the activity share of the monitored entities. Typical examples include operating-system maintained per-process resource utilisation statistics such as CPU utilisation, network throughput, or GPU driver statistics. These approaches are straightforward to implement and induce little overhead. Their accuracy, however, depends on how well the selected proxy correlates with the actual hardware activity. The activity share is estimated as

    \begin{equation}
    A_i=\frac{P_i}{\sum_j P_j},
    \end{equation}

    where $P_i$ denotes the selected activity proxy for software entity $i$.

    \item \emph{Model-based attribution} first reconstructs the activity proxy from multiple low-level execution statistics, such as Hardware Performance Counters (HPCs), before performing energy attribution. These statistics can be combined through analytical or learned models, for example regression models trained offline. We distinguish this class because, similarly to statistical sampling, it introduces an additional inference step between metric collection and energy allocation. The activity share is estimated as

    \begin{equation}
    A_i=\frac{f(\mathbf{P}_i)}
    {\sum_j f(\mathbf{P}_j)},
    \end{equation}

    where $\mathbf{P}_i$ denotes the vector of execution statistics associated with software entity $i$, and $f(\cdot)$ the reconstruction model.

    \item \emph{Statistical sampling} periodically samples execution state information, typically the process call stack, to estimate which code regions were active during execution. The relative frequency with which functions appear in the collected samples is then used to estimate their share of the process activity before distributing process-level energy accordingly. These approaches avoid explicit code instrumentation, but their accuracy depends on the sampling frequency and the representativeness of the collected samples. The activity share is estimated as

    \begin{equation}
    A_i=\frac{N_i}{\sum_j N_j},
    \end{equation}

    where $N_i$ denotes the number of samples in which software entity $i$ was observed.

    \item \emph{Execution markers} explicitly delimit execution regions using time markers inserted manually or automatically into the application. Process-level energy consumption is subsequently distributed across the execution intervals defined by these markers. Such approaches can provide precise fine-grained attribution, but generally require source-code modification, bytecode instrumentation, or runtime instrumentation, thereby increasing deployment complexity and runtime overhead. The activity share is estimated as

    \begin{equation}
    A_i=
    \frac{\sum_{k\in\mathcal{I}_i}\Delta t_k}
    {\sum_k \Delta t_k},
    \end{equation}

    where $\mathcal{I}_i$ denotes the set of execution intervals associated with software entity $i$, and $\Delta t_k$ the duration of interval $k$.

\end{itemize}

    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.8\columnwidth]{img/call-stack-and-time-markers.png}
    \caption{Principle of statistical call-stack sampling and execution time markers. Statistical sampling periodically captures the process call stack, using the relative frequency with which a function appears in the collected samples to estimate its share of the process activity, and proportionally attribute process-level energy. Execution-marker approaches instead instrument code to record the start and end of execution intervals, over which the measured process energy is accumulated and assigned to the corresponding code sections.}
    \label{fig:call-stack-sampling-and-time-markers}
\end{figure}

These estimation strategies represent different trade-offs between attribution fidelity, runtime overhead, portability, and deployment complexity.

\paragraph{\textbf{Baseline modelling and calibration}}

Even when no monitored application is running, computing systems still consume energy due to the operating system, background services, and idle hardware activity. Profiling tools therefore differ in how they account for this baseline consumption before attribution. Three main strategies can be distinguished.

\begin{itemize}

\item \emph{Dynamic-only attribution} removes the baseline energy before attribution and distributes only the dynamic consumption across the monitored entities:

\begin{equation}
E_i = E_{\mathrm{dynamic}} A_i,
\end{equation}

where

\[
E_{\mathrm{dynamic}} = E_{\mathrm{tot}} - E_{\mathrm{idle}}.
\]

\item \emph{Whole-system attribution} does not distinguish baseline and dynamic energy, and allocates the total measured energy proportionally:

\begin{equation}
E_i = E_{\mathrm{tot}} A_i.
\end{equation}

\item \emph{Separate baseline attribution} explicitly distinguishes idle and dynamic energy, allocating each component independently:

\begin{equation}
E_i =
E_{\mathrm{dynamic}}A_i^{\mathrm{dyn}}
+
E_{\mathrm{idle}}A_i^{\mathrm{idle}},
\end{equation}

where the activity shares used for dynamic and idle energy may differ depending on the profiling tool.
\end{itemize}

Estimating $E_{\mathrm{idle}}$ generally relies on one of three approaches: profiling the system while idle over a short period, estimating the active-to-idle ratio from system activity indicators such as overall CPU utilisation, or approximating the baseline as the minimum observed system power.

These choices can significantly influence attribution results, particularly when idle or background consumption represents a substantial fraction of the total system power.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Virtualised environment support}}

Virtualisation introduces additional attribution challenges because applications running inside virtual machines generally do not have direct access to the usual hardware-level energy estimation sources exposed on the host system.

As a result, profiling tools supporting virtualised environments typically rely on two main approaches. Some use pre-trained or calibrated models to estimate the energy share of the virtual machine from observable guest-level activity metrics such as CPU utilisation, allocated cores and memory. Others deploy monitoring components on the host system capable of estimating virtual-machine-level energy consumption, then expose these estimations to the guest environment through shared files.

Some tools targeting code-section-level attribution, rely on another profiler to obtain process-level attribution before refining the attribution within the application itself. Their support for virtualised environments therefore depends on the virtualisation support provided by the process-level profiler.

These two approaches differ in how they trade portability for accuracy and dependence on the host environment. Model-based approaches are more portable and do not require host operators to explicitly expose energy measurements to guest environments, but they introduce additional modelling uncertainties. Conversely, host-assisted approaches may provide more accurate estimations, at the cost of stronger dependencies on the underlying infrastructure and monitoring stack.

\subsection{Operational constraints}

Operational constraints describe the practical conditions and limitations under which profiling tools operate. They are strongly influenced by the underlying design choices and characterise the deployability, portability, and runtime implications of profiling approaches in real execution environments.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Temporal resolution}}

Temporal resolution represents the frequency at which measurements are collected. Higher sampling frequencies improve visibility into fast events, but increase runtime overhead, storage requirements, and sensitivity to noise.
Some tools allow temporal resolution to be configured, but the achievable range mainly depends on the underlying measurement sources. Both hardware-level energy estimation and software activity proxies expose metrics at finite update rates, and the effective temporal resolution is therefore bounded by the slowest. For hardware-level energy estimation, external power meters provide the highest sampling frequencies. 
Hardware-exposed interfaces usually offer a good compromise between resolution and overhead, often operating at frequencies on the order of a kilo hertz.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Runtime overhead}}

Profiling inevitably perturbs the monitored system. Overhead originates from the profiling tool own activity including instrumentation logic, metric collection and processing, storage operations, etc\dots. It can be determinant when monitored workloads are lightweight.

Different levers exist to reduce the overhead depending on the measurement mechanism. Common approaches include reducing the acquisition frequency, performing attribution or analysis offline rather than online, or selecting lower-overhead operating-system interfaces to access measurement sources. For example, \cite{raffinDissectingSoftwarebasedMeasurement2024} showed that different Linux interfaces used to access RAPL counters exhibit significantly different latency and energy overhead characteristics, especially at high acquisition frequencies or on idle systems.

Some tools provide mechanisms to estimate profiling overhead. However, overhead evaluation is most often reported separately in validation studies. When available, we reference these studies in the taxonomy table.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Code modification}}

Some profiling tools operate transparently from outside the application, while others require code instrumentation through either manual source-code annotations exposing execution time markers or automatic bytecode rewriting performed by runtime agents.

Manual instrumentation allows developers to precisely target the code sections of interest. However, it requires explicit human intervention, making it impractical for large projects or automated monitoring of arbitrary code. Automatic instrumentation addresses this issue by inserting the required instrumentation at build time or runtime, although it typically requires rebuilding the application and may introduce additional deployment complexity.

When code instrumentation is required, we indicate it and distinguish between manual instrumentation performed by the developer and automatic instrumentation carried out by the profiling tool.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Privilege requirements}}

Profiling tools also differ in the privileges required to access measurement sources. Some operate entirely in user space using standard operating-system interfaces (e.g., PAPI), while others require elevated privileges to access restricted hardware interfaces such as RAPL Model-Specific Registers (MSRs) or Hardware Performance Counters (HPCs). Certain approaches also rely on kernel-level facilities, such as eBPF programs, kprobes, or dedicated kernel modules, whose deployment generally requires administrative privileges.

Privilege requirements directly impact deployability, particularly in environments where users may lack administrative access. We therefore distinguish between tools that \emph{(i) operate entirely in user space}, \emph{(ii) require elevated privileges to access measurement interfaces}, and \emph{(iii) depend on additional kernel-level instrumentation or modules}.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Platform dependency}}

Profiling tools may target specific hardware architectures, operating systems, execution environments, or programming languages. Dependencies may originate from vendor-specific hardware-exposed energy interfaces, kernel APIs, instrumentation frameworks, or language-specific runtime mechanisms.

The taxonomy therefore distinguishes support for desktop systems, servers, single-board computers (e.g., Raspberry Pi), x86, ARM, and Apple Silicon platforms. For fine-grained code-section attribution, we additionally specify language dependencies when profiling mechanisms rely on language-specific instrumentation frameworks or runtime agents, such as Java bytecode instrumentation.

We additionally specify whether profiling tools can operate inside virtual machines or containers, and whether they provide Kubernetes integration. These aspects naturally relate to both attribution granularity and virtualised environment support. In cloud-native environments, Kubernetes manages workload scheduling and resource allocation in a role conceptually similar to that of an operating system. Energy profiling at pod or container level therefore becomes particularly valuable, as it enables orchestration decisions such as scheduling or scaling to consider energy-related metrics.

\subsection{Maturity and ecosystem}

Under this dimension, we provide additional information that does not directly influence the profiling methodology itself, but reflects the maturity, usability, and practical adoption of the investigated tools.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Deployment complexity}}

Deployment complexity reflects the effort required to configure and deploy the profiling tools. This may include dependency installation, privilege configuration, or calibration procedures.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Accuracy validation}}

When available, we provide references to studies evaluating the accuracy of the profiling tools. These studies typically compare the reported measurements against external power meters or other reference measurements.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Availability and licensing}}

For each tool, we indicate whether it is publicly available and whether its source code is open-source.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Maintenance status}}

We report the presence of recent development activity as an indicator of the tool's current maintenance status.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Case studies}}

When available, we provide references to publicly available case studies using the profiling tools. These case studies provide additional insight into the operational contexts, capabilities, and limitations of the tools.

\subsubsection{\textbf{Additional metrics}}

Finally, some tools expose metrics beyond direct energy estimation, including network I/O, disk I/O, carbon-intensity estimation, or embodied-emission allocation. These additional indicators may support broader sustainability analyses but also introduce additional modelling assumptions beyond power measurement itself.


\subsection{Discussion}

The proposed taxonomy characterise the challenges introduced in the previous section into observable dimensions enabling systematic comparison between profiling approaches.

Importantly, these dimensions are strongly interdependent. For instance:
\begin{itemize}
    \item fine-grained attribution generally requires more intrusive activity sources and increases runtime overhead,
    \item support for virtualised environments constrains the visibility of measurement sources,
    \item model-based approaches improve portability but may reduce attribution accuracy,
    \item low-level observability mechanisms may require elevated privileges and reduce deployability.
\end{itemize}

Profiling tools can therefore be understood as different compromises within this multidimensional design space. The comparative analysis presented later uses this taxonomy to identify recurring architectural patterns, implicit assumptions, and trade-offs across existing approaches.


\section{Positioning state-of-the-art tools}

\begin{figure}[p]
    \centering
    \includegraphics[width=0.77\paperheight,angle=90]{img/designspace.png}
    \caption{Positioning of the state-of-the-art monitoring tools in the design space}
    \label{fig:design-space}
\end{figure}

Our analysis shows that software energy profiling tools have gradually specialised towards three distinct application domains.

The first category targets cloud-native environments. These tools support virtualised workloads through container- or virtual machine-level attribution and integrate with orchestration frameworks such as Kubernetes. As Kubernetes increasingly acts as the operating system of cloud-native applications, exposing energy metrics at the orchestration level enables scheduling, optimisation and monitoring decisions that were previously impossible. Recent frameworks such as GreenCube illustrate this trend by integrating energy-awareness directly into cluster management.

The second category focuses on process-level attribution. These tools estimate the energy consumption of running applications with little or no application modification, making them suitable for software benchmarking. Several recent tools also extend this capability across multiple operating systems. For example, PowerJoular and Joular Core \cite{noureddinePowerJoularJoularJXMultiPlatform2022} broaden platform support beyond Linux, allowing developers to compare the energy behaviour of the same application across heterogeneous hardware and operating systems. Such portability is particularly valuable when evaluating implementation choices. For instance, a Java implementation may be more energy efficient than its Python equivalent on one platform while the opposite may hold on another.

The third category targets software development and code optimisation. Rather than attributing energy to complete processes, these tools estimate the consumption of functions or user-defined code regions to help developers identify optimisation opportunities. As far as we know, language-independent support for code-region attribution remains very limited. The Green Metrics Tool \cite{DocsgreencodingioDocsPrologue} provides such functionality by combining container isolation, container-level attribution and execution time markers. However, developers must manually instrument the application by placing these markers, making the approach semi-automatic. We recently applied this methodology to evaluate the energy consumption of simulated user interactions with webmail front-ends \cite{kayembeExploringPrivacySecurity2025}. Among language-specific solutions, JoularJX \cite{noureddinePowerJoularJoularJXMultiPlatform2022} currently provides one of the most complete solutions for Java applications.

Despite their differences, nearly all recent tools rely on Intel RAPL whenever hardware support is available. Earlier work often relied on analytical models to estimate CPU energy because hardware counters were unavailable or inaccessible. Today, modelling is primarily used for a different purpose: enabling attribution in virtualised environments where direct hardware measurements cannot be assigned to individual containers or virtual machines. These models are increasingly data-driven and architecture-specific rather than analytical.

Although model-based attribution currently provides a practical compromise for cloud environments, we believe that cloud providers could significantly improve measurement accuracy by exposing trusted energy metrics directly to guest virtual machines or containers. Hypervisors already observe the physical hardware and execute the attribution mechanisms required to isolate workloads. Exposing these measurements would eliminate the need for guest-side estimation models while enabling consistent and accurate software energy profiling across cloud platforms.

Finally, our analysis highlights several gaps in the current state of the art. Most tools remain Linux-centric despite recent efforts to improve portability. Very few tools consider hardware components beyond the CPU and DRAM. Among the analysed tools, only e-Surgeon \cite{noureddineMonitoringEnergyHotspots2015} explicitly incorporates network activity, while none provides meaningful storage energy attribution. Thread-level attribution also remains largely unexplored. Existing attempts, such as the evaluation of IgProf \cite{khanEnergyProfilingUsing2015}, report limited accuracy, suggesting that reliable fine-grained attribution below the process level remains an open research challenge.
